= Resource access

[.lead]
An application can only do what it has been given.

That is the whole rule. Everything in this chapter follows from it, and if some
part of the platform ever seems to contradict it, the platform is wrong.

Most of what you build needs nothing from this chapter. A window, a layout, a
timer, your own state: none of it touches anything belonging to anyone else, so
none of it needs permission. This chapter is about the moment your application
wants something that belongs to the person using it — their documents, their
network, their devices, their clipboard.

== What you start with

Every application begins with a useful private environment, provided
automatically and without a prompt:

* a window, its input, and a clock;
* private storage of your own, isolated from every other application;
* the assets you shipped inside your own executable.

None of these holds anyone's data but yours, and none of them can reach anything
else, which is exactly why they need no decision from anybody. An application
that uses only these never asks the person for anything, and should not.

== How you get more

Someone gives it to you. Usually that someone is the person using your
application, and the way they give it to you is by *choosing the thing*.

[source,roc]
----
access.pick_directory!()
----

This opens the operating system's own folder chooser. If the person picks a
folder, you are handed a value that reads that folder. If they cancel, you are
handed nothing, and cancelling is an ordinary outcome rather than a failure.

When one file is all you need, ask for one file:

[source,roc]
----
access.pick_file!([{ label: "Captures", extensions: ["rgstats"], mime_types: [] }])
----

The person picks a file of a type you offered, and you are handed a
`Gui.FilesFileRead` that reads that file and nothing beside it: not its
siblings, not its folder. Ask for the smallest thing that does the job; a
document viewer that asks for a folder has asked for every other document in
it.

There is no second step. No "this application would like to access your files"
dialog appears before or after, because the choosing *was* the permission.
Someone who picked `~/Photos/2026` has said everything that needs saying, and
asking them again only teaches them to click past questions without reading.

That is the practical point. The usual way a permission system fails is that
people stop reading it. Every decision this platform asks for is attached to a
thing the person just picked, shown in a window they recognise, at the moment
they wanted something to happen.

== A grant is a value

What you get back is an ordinary Roc value. You hold it in your state, pass it
to functions, and store it in a record:

[source,roc]
----
State : { project : [None, Some(Gui.FilesDirRead)] }
----

Three things are true of it, and between them they are the reason this works.

*You cannot make one up.* There is no constructor. A `Gui.FilesDirRead` exists
because someone handed you one.

*Its type says what it is for.* A `Gui.FilesDirRead` reads a directory. There is
no permission list to consult alongside it, because the type you are holding is
the permission, and at every call site you can see exactly what you are handing
over.

*Nothing is ambient.* No function anywhere in the platform conjures authority
out of nothing. Every grant you hold traces back to something a person chose, or
to the small private environment above.

== Narrowing, and handing things on

You can always give away less than you hold, and never more.

[source,roc]
----
covers = project.open_dir!("covers")
----

`covers` reads one subfolder. It cannot see its parent, its siblings, or
anything above it. When some part of your application needs to work with the
covers, give it `covers` and not `project`.

This is the part that protects *you*, rather than the person using your
application.

A Roc package you depend on is code you did not write, compiled into your
binary. In most ecosystems such a package holds everything your program holds:
it can read any file your process can read and open any connection your process
can open. The attacks that actually happen to working developers are
overwhelmingly this shape.

Here a library can use only what you hand it. A charting package that takes your
data and returns an element cannot read your documents, because you never gave
it anything that reads documents and it has no way to ask for one. You do not
have to audit it to know that — you can see what it received.

[NOTE]
====
This bounds the blast radius; it is not a guarantee of good behaviour. A library
you hand a network client to may use that client for anything. Give the narrowest
thing that does the job, and notice when a library asks for something broad.
====

== Taking it back

A person can withdraw a grant. The trusted *App access* surface lists what this
application is holding — what each grant is over, how it arrived, and what it
allows — with a control to withdraw each one. It belongs to the platform rather
than to the application: the application cannot draw it, suppress it, or tell
that it is open, which is the only way a withdrawal control is worth anything.

When a grant is withdrawn, everything derived from it stops at the same moment:
the subfolder handles, the database opened from that folder, the watch waiting
for that database to change, the connection opened through that device. You do
not have to hunt them down and close them. They refuse their next use with a
typed error that says the authority was withdrawn, rather than one that says
something went wrong, and a watch that is waiting wakes to say so.

Two limits, stated plainly because a model that overpromises here is worse than
one that does not try:

* Work already finished stays finished. Bytes you have read are in your
  application's memory, and withdrawing a grant cannot reach in and remove them.
* A grant given for a session ends with the session. Nothing carries over to the
  next run unless the person asked for it to be remembered.

== Why it is built this way

The idea is older than most of the software that ignores it.

In 1966 Dennis and Van Horn described a *capability*: an unforgeable reference
that both names a resource and carries the right to use it. The essential
property is the absence of ambient authority. You cannot act on something merely
because you know its name.

In 1988 Norm Hardy wrote up the *confused deputy*, still the clearest argument
for why that matters. A compiler on a shared system could write to a billing file
its users could not. A user asked the compiler to write output to that path, and
the compiler — holding authority the user lacked — destroyed the billing records.
Nothing was broken into. Access control lists answer "who is asking", and the
answer was "the compiler", which was true and useless. Capabilities answer a
better question: what did the requester bring with them?

File paths are that problem's natural habitat. A path is a name anyone can write
down, and whether it works depends on who happens to be running. Hand over a
directory handle instead and the question cannot arise.

The *powerbox* — a trusted chooser where the act of choosing is the act of
granting — came out of the E language and CapDesk in the early 2000s, and is now
the mainstream answer rather than an exotic one. WebAssembly's WASI has no
ambient `open`: a module receives directory handles or reaches nothing. Deno made
permission explicit and per-resource. XDG Desktop Portals on Linux, and the file
and photo pickers on macOS and iOS, are the same shape — the operating system
shows the chooser and the application receives only what was chosen.

The counter-example is worth keeping in mind too. Android first showed a list of
permissions at install time, and people learned to scroll past it, because a list
of abstract capabilities presented before anything has happened is not a question
anyone can answer. Every platform that started there has since moved the decision
to the moment of use and attached it to the thing being used.

== What this costs you

It would be dishonest to present this as free.

* You have to thread grants through your application state. A function that
  reads a file needs the handle passed to it, so your state has to hold it and
  your call sites have to show it.
* You cannot open a path you were not given, including for good reasons. A
  convenience you might expect — reading a configuration file from a known
  location — is private storage here instead.
* Development and automation need grants with nobody present to give them. Those
  flags exist, and they are recorded as provisioning rather than as consent, so
  evidence never reports a test fixture as a decision a person made.
* It does not make untrusted *data* safe. A malformed image is dangerous to
  decode however narrowly you were granted the file it arrived in.

If a rule in this chapter costs you something and protects nobody, that is a
defect in the platform, and worth saying so.

== What is deliberately absent

* No permission list in a manifest, and no approval at install time.
* No "allow this application to access your files" prompt, before or after a
  choice.
* No interface that takes an absolute path.
* No switch that grants everything, for convenience or for compatibility.

The last is the load-bearing absence. A model with an escape hatch becomes a
model everybody escapes, and then the sentence at the top of this chapter stops
being true.
